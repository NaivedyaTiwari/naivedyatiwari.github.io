04 case studies

Four decisions from Jeeva AI, an agentic AI sales platform. Each one includes what the data said, what we changed, and where I was wrong.

01 Finding the real cause of churn Retention · Jeeva AI · 2026

The problem

Users were signing up and not coming back. The working assumption across the team was that onboarding was too long.

What I did

Rather than act on the assumption, I pulled event data from PostHog and GA4 alongside MongoDB queries on user records, and grouped people by where they stopped: signed up but never activated, activated once and never returned, and genuinely recurring.

Two separate causes showed up. The core agentic feature was failing in production, so users who did reach it got an unreliable experience and left. Separately, onboarding was long enough that a large share never reached the product at all.

What changed

I ran failure-mode analysis on the agent and took the findings to engineering so we fixed causes rather than symptoms. In parallel we cut onboarding down to a single agentic prompt, so new users hit value immediately.

Result. Reported issues fell 45–50%. AI feature adoption grew 35–40%.

What the assumption missed

The team was half right. Onboarding was a genuine problem, but fixing it alone would have left the larger one untouched, and we would have concluded the fix had failed.

02 Repricing credits against real consumption Pricing & unit economics · Jeeva AI · 2026

The problem

Conversion was healthy but the unit economics were not, and I suspected the credits bundled at each price point were wrong.

What I did

I tracked signup-to-conversion in PostHog and pulled consumption data from existing product-led customers to model how fast people actually burned credits. That gave a baseline for what a customer genuinely costs per package.

The finding: credits were too low for what people were paying. Users were hitting limits and stalling, which was damaging retention as much as revenue.

What changed

We raised prices while tripling the credit allocation, on the reasoning that heavier usage would not hurt margin much at the higher tier, since consumption data showed most users never approached the new ceiling. We also made the credit card mandatory and pushed toward annual plans. I ran it as an A/B test on pricing, with a separate with-card versus without-card test on signup.

Result. Signups held. Unit economics came in below forecast.

Where the model was wrong

My consumption assumption came from older cohorts, and usage at the new tier ran heavier than they predicted. I reported the gap rather than the headline, and we adjusted pricing from there.

03 Defining what “correct” means for a probabilistic product AI evaluation · Jeeva AI · 2025–2026

The problem

Agentic AI does not return the same answer twice. Without an agreed definition of good, every release argument becomes a matter of opinion and the loudest bug wins.

What I did

I built an evaluation framework around three measures with baselines and acceptance bands: hallucination rate, latency, and context loss across multi-turn interactions. These became release gates rather than advisory metrics.

Underneath that: prompt and prompt-chain testing, scenario testing against real user conversation flows, and RAG retrieval validation against a chunked Notion and Confluence knowledge base, checking that answers were grounded in the correct source rather than plausible but unsupported. Failures were traced through LangSmith and system data to separate model limitations from retrieval, context handling and integration problems.

Result. Reported issues fell 45–50%, with recurring failure modes documented for engineering rather than rediscovered each sprint.

The hard part

Measuring is the easy half. Agreeing what “correct” means when there is no single right answer is the actual work, and it is a product decision rather than a technical one.

04 Pulling a feature after it shipped Post-implementation review · Jeeva AI · 2026

The problem

We shipped a memory feature so the agent could carry context about users between sessions.

What I found

In post-implementation review it became clear that users had no way to correct or edit what the system had stored about them. A wrong inference stayed wrong, and kept shaping every later interaction.

What I did

I judged that structural rather than something iteration would fix, and made the case to pull it and rebuild rather than keep spending engineering time on the version we had.

Result. The feature came out. That engineering time went to work with a clearer payoff.

What I would change

This should have surfaced in design review, not after launch. “What happens when the model gets this wrong, and can the user fix it?” is now a question I ask before building anything that stores inferences about people.
